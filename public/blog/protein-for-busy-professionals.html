Protein for Busy Professionals: A Practical Floor, Not a Diet

By Nemezio LopezPerez · October 24, 2026 · 7 min read

You probably need more protein than you are eating, but you do not need a diet to get it. Think of protein as a floor: a palm-sized portion at each meal, three or four times a day, drawn from a short list of foods you already like. Then give each of those meals a fallback version for travel days and back-to-back meetings, so the floor holds when the week does not.

Most busy professionals who track their food for a few days are surprised by the same thing. Breakfast is coffee, lunch is whatever is closest to the desk, and most of the day's protein arrives at dinner. Strength training asks the body to build and keep muscle, and that work needs raw material spread through the day, not packed into one evening meal.

Why a floor works better than a diet

A diet is a set of rules about what you cannot have. It works on calm weeks and collapses on the first client dinner. A floor is a single minimum you hit, then eat around however you like. It asks for one thing, it is easy to check, and it crowds out a fair amount of the snacking that happens when meals are light.

Protein is the best candidate for a floor for three reasons:

  • It protects the training investment. If you are lifting two or three times a week, protein is what lets that work turn into muscle you keep, especially while losing fat.
  • It is the most filling part of a meal. Meals built around protein tend to hold you longer, which makes the rest of the day's eating easier to manage without counting everything.
  • It is the part busy schedules squeeze out first. Grab-and-go food is mostly carbohydrate and fat. Protein usually takes a deliberate choice, which is exactly why it needs a plan.

How much is enough to start

Your exact number depends on your body size, how much you train and what you are training toward, and it is worth setting properly with a coach. But you do not need the exact number to start. A practical starting point many coaches use is one to two palm-sized portions of protein per meal, roughly 25 to 40 grams, three or four times a day. Larger, more active people sit at the top of that range; smaller people at the bottom.

The palm is the useful part. It travels with you, it scales with your body, and it works in a restaurant where nobody is weighing anything. A cooked chicken breast or salmon fillet about the size and thickness of your palm lands near the middle of that range.

Inside our nutrition coaching, the protein target is set against your training load and body composition baseline, then adjusted as the numbers move. The floor above is where most people begin before that adjustment.

Reading the protein chart

The chart at the top of this article lists nine common sources and roughly how much protein each provides:

  • Chicken breast: about 31 g per 100 g
  • Lean beef: about 26 g per 100 g
  • Whey protein: about 24 g per scoop
  • Salmon: about 22 g per 100 g
  • Lentils: about 18 g per cup, cooked
  • Greek yogurt: about 17 g per cup
  • Cottage cheese: about 14 g per half cup
  • Tofu: about 10 g per 100 g
  • Eggs: about 6 g each

Two things to notice. First, the serving units differ: grams for meat and fish, cups for dairy and lentils, a scoop for whey, one egg at a time. Do not compare the headline numbers directly; ask how much of each you would actually eat in a sitting. Two eggs alone give you about 12 g, which is why eggs work best paired with something else, such as Greek yogurt or a slice of smoked salmon.

Second, every figure is approximate. Brands, cuts, serving sizes and cooking all move the number. That is fine. A floor does not need precision to work; it needs to happen most days. If you want an exact figure for a packaged food, read its label.

Build needs an Adaptation twin

The OmniFit Method runs a physical track and a behavioral track side by side. On the physical side, the Build stage is where progressive strength training and body recomposition happen, and protein is part of what makes that training pay off. On the behavioral side, Build is paired with Adaptation: making habits hold up under real-life pressure such as travel, deadlines and client dinners.

That pairing is the whole argument of this article. Knowing your protein target is the easy part. Hitting it on the Tuesday you fly to Chicago, sit in meetings until three and eat dinner at a client's choice of restaurant is the hard part. In our behavior change coaching, each habit gets a minimum version, so a bad week bends the plan instead of breaking it.

Minimum versions for real weeks

Pick one default and one fallback for each meal. Write them down once, then stop deciding every day.

  • Breakfast at home: Greek yogurt with berries, or two or three eggs with cottage cheese on the side. Fallback: a whey shake made in the time the coffee takes.
  • Lunch at the desk: a salad or bowl with a full palm of chicken, salmon or beef added. Fallback: a pre-cooked chicken breast and a piece of fruit kept in the office fridge.
  • Back-to-back meeting days: a cottage cheese or Greek yogurt cup between calls. It counts as a meal if it carries the protein.
  • Travel days: airport and hotel menus almost always offer eggs, Greek yogurt or a grilled protein. Order the protein first, then build the rest of the meal around it.
  • Client dinners: choose the fish, steak or chicken entree and enjoy the rest of the meal. One protein-led choice keeps the floor without anyone noticing.
  • Plant-based days: lentils, tofu and Greek yogurt or cottage cheese if you eat dairy. Plant portions run larger for the same protein, so plan the extra volume.

None of these is a meal plan. Each is a pre-made decision that removes the moment where a busy day talks you out of it.

OmniFit Performance coaches protein as a daily floor rather than a diet. Clients start with one to two palm-sized portions of protein at each of three or four meals, then give every meal a fallback version for travel days, long meeting blocks and client dinners. The goal is a minimum that holds on the hardest weeks, adjusted against training load and body composition over time.

Check it like any other number

A floor only works if you know whether you are hitting it. For two weeks, keep a one-line log: how many meals today carried a full palm of protein, out of the three or four you planned. No calorie counting, no weighing food.

That count usually shows the problem quickly. For most people it is one specific meal, often breakfast or a late afternoon gap, not the whole day. Changing that one meal's default is a much smaller job than overhauling everything.

If fat loss is part of your goal, protein matters even more, because you want the weight that comes off to be fat rather than hard-earned muscle. Our fat loss program pairs calorie and protein targets with strength training for that reason, and tracks body composition rather than the scale alone.

When to check with a clinician first

If you have kidney disease or another medical condition, follow a prescribed diet, or take medication that affects your appetite, talk to your physician or a registered dietitian before raising your protein intake. OmniFit Performance is a personal training practice, not a medical provider. We coach eating habits within whatever your clinician advises, and we refer to a licensed clinician when a question goes beyond coaching.

Common Questions

Do I need protein shakes to hit my target?

No. Whole foods can cover it, and they keep you fuller. A shake is simply a convenient fallback for the mornings or travel days when a real meal is not going to happen, which is how we use them.

Can I get enough protein without eating meat?

Yes, with more planning. Lentils, tofu, eggs and dairy all contribute, but plant sources usually provide less protein per serving, so portions are larger and combining sources across the day matters more.

Do I have to eat protein right after a workout?

For most people, the total across the day and spreading it over several meals matter more than a narrow window after training. If your next meal is a few hours away, having it closer to the session is a reasonable habit, not a rule.

Is eating more protein safe?

Raising protein toward the floor described here is a common coaching starting point for active adults. If you have kidney concerns or another medical condition, ask your physician before changing your intake, and we will set your targets within their guidance.
