If you are losing weight on a GLP-1 medication, the scale tells you how much is coming off but not what it is made of. To know you are keeping your muscle, track four numbers alongside it: estimated lean mass from a repeatable body composition scan, a few strength benchmarks, your daily protein, and how many planned strength sessions actually happened. Read together every few weeks, they show the difference between losing weight and losing the right weight.

Most people on semaglutide or tirzepatide watch one number, and for good reason: it moves. But a falling scale weight is a total. It adds together fat, muscle, water and everything else, and it cannot tell you which part is leaving. On a medication that reduces appetite this much, that blind spot matters more than usual.

Why the scale is the wrong scoreboard on medication

Published research on GLP-1 medications has found that a meaningful share of the weight lost can come from lean mass rather than fat, and that much of the weight tends to return when people stop. Neither finding is a prediction about you, and neither is an argument against the medication. They are an argument for watching the right numbers while you are on it.

Muscle is the part of your body you most want to keep. It is what lets you carry luggage through an airport, get up off the floor easily and keep training as the years go on. When you are eating far less than usual, the body has less reason to hold on to it unless training gives it one. The scale will not tell you whether that is happening. The four numbers below will.

Number one: estimated lean mass, measured the same way each time

A body composition scan splits your weight into fat mass and lean mass. That split is the single most useful thing to know on a GLP-1, because it answers the question the scale cannot: is the weight coming off the weight you wanted to lose?

Two cautions keep this number honest. First, bioelectrical impedance devices estimate lean mass from how current travels through the body; they do not measure muscle directly. Second, readings shift with hydration, a recent meal or a hard workout. So the reading that matters is not any single scan but the trend across several, taken under the same conditions: same device, same time of day, same hydration routine, before training rather than after.

What to look for: fat mass trending down while estimated lean mass holds roughly steady. A small dip in lean mass is common early, partly from water. A steady slide across several scans is the signal to change the plan. Our body composition testing uses the same multi-frequency device and protocol at every rescan for exactly this reason.

Number two: strength on a few key lifts

Scans happen every few weeks. Strength shows up in every session, which makes it the fastest early warning you have.

Pick three or four movements you train regularly, such as a squat or leg press, a hinge like a deadlift, a press and a row, and record the load and repetitions each time. You are not chasing personal records while eating this little. The goal is simpler: are you holding the weights you could lift before you started the medication, or close to them?

Holding strength through a large calorie deficit is a good sign that muscle is being kept. Strength that falls week after week, across several lifts, is worth taking seriously even if your energy explains part of it. Grip strength is a useful extra: quick to test, easy to repeat and hard to fake.

Number three: protein, counted as a daily floor

Appetite suppression is the point of the medication, and it is also what makes eating enough protein hard. You are not going to eat more food. The question is what goes into the smaller amount you do eat.

Treat protein as a floor rather than a diet: a minimum you hit every day, then eat around. Skip calorie counting for this one. Simply record how many meals carried a real portion of protein, out of the three or four you planned. If you want the full approach, our earlier article on protein as a practical floor for busy professionals sets it out, and your exact target is worth setting with a coach against your training and your prescriber's guidance.

On days when appetite is very low, protein comes first. Eat it before the rest of the plate, choose dense sources over bulky ones, and treat a yogurt cup or a shake as a meal if it carries the protein.

Number four: the sessions that actually happened

Resistance training is what gives the body a reason to keep its muscle. So the fourth number is not about how hard you trained; it is whether you trained. Count planned strength sessions against completed ones each week: two of two, two of three, one of three.

This number is the context for the other three. If lean mass slipped during a month where you completed most of your sessions and hit your protein floor, the plan needs to change. If it slipped during a month of travel where training mostly did not happen, the plan may be fine and the week was not. Without this count, you cannot tell those two stories apart.

Reading the four numbers together

No single number is the verdict. The pattern across all four is. A few common ones:

  • Fat mass down, lean mass steady, strength holding: the plan is working. Keep doing what you are doing.
  • Lean mass sliding, strength falling, sessions and protein on target: the training or eating needs to change. This is the conversation to have with your coach, and the data worth sharing with your prescriber.
  • Lean mass sliding, sessions or protein missed: the plan may be fine. The job is making it survivable on hard weeks, with a minimum session and a fallback protein meal.
  • Strength dipping, one scan looks off, everything else steady: wait for the next scan. Check sleep, hydration and the timing of the scan before reading too much into one result.

Common Questions

Is losing some muscle on a GLP-1 unavoidable?

Some change in lean mass is common during significant weight loss, and part of an early dip is usually water. What you can influence is how much: regular resistance training and enough protein are the two levers within your control. Tracking lean mass over time is how you find out whether they are working.

How often should I get a body composition scan?

Every four to eight weeks is a practical rhythm for most people. Scanning more often mostly measures day-to-day water changes, not real shifts. Keep the conditions the same each time so the trend means something.

Can I just use my smart scale's body fat reading?

It can show a rough direction, but single-frequency home scales are sensitive to hydration and tend to be less consistent. If muscle is the question, a repeatable scan on the same device under the same conditions is the better tool. Either way, track the trend rather than any single reading.

Should I start strength training before or after I begin the medication?

Either works, but earlier is better, because it gives you a baseline for strength and lean mass before the weight starts moving. If you are already months in, start now and take a scan first so you have a reference point. Check with your physician before starting any new training program.
