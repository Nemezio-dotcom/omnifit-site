HSA & FSA Eligibility

Is Personal Training HSA or FSA Eligible?

Yes, in some cases. Personal training can be paid for with HSA or FSA funds when a licensed provider issues a Letter of Medical Necessity stating that the training is intended to treat, mitigate, or prevent a specific diagnosed condition. Training for general fitness, appearance, or overall wellbeing does not qualify under IRS rules. Eligibility is determined by a licensed provider, not by a trainer.

The Rule

What the IRS Actually Requires

The IRS treats an expense as medical when it is incurred primarily to alleviate or prevent a physical or mental defect or illness. Expenses that are merely beneficial to general health do not qualify, and the guidance names gym dues and fitness programs explicitly as examples of the latter.

That distinction is the entire mechanism. The same session, with the same coach, at the same price, is either a personal expense or a medical one depending on whether a licensed provider has documented that it addresses a diagnosed condition. The document that records this is a Letter of Medical Necessity.

A Letter of Medical Necessity names the condition, the recommended intervention, and the duration for which it applies. Your plan administrator uses it to decide whether to pay the claim.

In Practice

What Tends to Qualify

The examples below come up frequently. None of them qualify automatically, and a licensed provider decides in every case.

Post-rehabilitation training

Structured strength work following discharge from physical therapy, where the goal is restoring function and reducing the chance of reinjury rather than general conditioning.

Chronic back or joint pain

Corrective exercise addressing a diagnosed musculoskeletal condition, where the training is documented as part of managing that condition.

Metabolic conditions

Diagnosed conditions such as type 2 diabetes, hypertension, or obesity where a provider has recommended supervised exercise as part of treatment.

Bone density

Resistance training recommended in response to a diagnosis of osteopenia or osteoporosis, where load-bearing exercise is part of the clinical plan.

Related

Are Gym Memberships HSA Eligible?

The mechanism is identical. Membership dues paid for general fitness are not a qualifying medical expense, and IRS guidance says so directly. With a Letter of Medical Necessity tying the membership to a diagnosed condition, it may qualify.

The practical difference is that a gym membership is harder to document as a specific intervention than supervised training is, because there is no record of what was actually done. Administrators vary in how they treat this.

Can I use an FSA for personal training?

FSA funds are typically use-it-or-lose-it within the plan year, so timing matters more than it does with an HSA.

What is a Letter of Medical Necessity?

A Letter of Medical Necessity, often shortened to LMN, is a document from a licensed provider stating that a specific expense is intended to treat, mitigate, or prevent a diagnosed medical condition. It names the condition, the recommended intervention, and the duration. Plan administrators use it to determine whether an expense qualifies as medical rather than personal.

Is a gym membership HSA eligible?

A gym membership follows the same mechanism as personal training. It is not eligible by default, but it may qualify with a Letter of Medical Necessity tying the membership to a diagnosed condition. Membership dues for general fitness are explicitly not a qualifying medical expense under IRS guidance.

After you submit the letter and an itemized receipt to your plan administrator, claims are commonly paid within two to four weeks. Timing depends entirely on your administrator, not on OmniFit Performance.
